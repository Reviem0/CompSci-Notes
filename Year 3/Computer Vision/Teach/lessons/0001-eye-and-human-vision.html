The Eye and Human Vision

Is human vision a good model for computer vision?

Why this matters for the exam This lecture is mostly descriptive, so exam questions on it are usually recall and short explanation. Typical forms: “describe the three-part model”, “explain Mach bands”, “what do illusions tell us?”, and the lecture's headline question, “is human vision a good model for computer vision?”
Your win for this lesson From memory, explain the three-part model of human vision. For each part, give one fact about how it works and one way it fails. Then use that to argue both sides of the headline question.

1 · Three models, one system

The lecture splits human vision into three parts. Each part matches one stage of the system:Slide 4, “Modelling the eye in three parts”. Nixon & Aguado, Feature Extraction and Image Processing for Computer Vision, 4th ed., pp. 1–28 (the course text).

ModelStageWhat it covers
Physicalthe eyeOptics and sensors: how the image forms on the retina
Experimentalthe signalsNeural processing: how sensor responses are coded and combined
Psychologicalthe brainPerception: how the cortex turns signals into what we “see”

None of the three is fully understood, and the brain is understood least. Keep that in mind, because it comes back in the headline question.

2 · The physical model: the eye

The eye evolved for survival. Its job is to form an image on the retina, and the sharpest part of that image lands on the fovea. Two details are easy exam marks:Ray diagram of the eye: HyperPhysics (linked from the slides).

  • The lens changes shape to focus. It does not move, whereas a camera lens moves.
  • The image on the retina is upside down, so the brain has to invert it. It then travels down the optic nerve.

There are no sensors at all on the blind spot, where the optic nerve leaves the eye.Rod and cone density plot from Webvision (Univ. of Utah), as shown on slide 9. There are three types of cone:

  • S: short wavelength (blue). Its response is poor.
  • M: medium wavelength (green). Its response dominates.
  • L: long wavelength (red). Its range sits close to heat (infrared).

Because the blue response is weak, the visual system has to compensate for it, much as a camera does. There is also a bandwidth problem: around 108 sensors feed one optic nerve, which can't carry all that raw data. The signal therefore has to be coded (compressed) before it leaves the eye.

3 · The experimental model: neural signals

The sensor outputs have to be combined. The lecture models this in three steps. First, each sensor input pi goes through a logarithmic response. Second, each result is weighted. Third, the weighted results are summed:

output = Σi wi · log(pi)

The log stage is the link to Weber's law: the smallest change in brightness you can notice is a fixed fraction of the background intensity (ΔI / I ≈ constant). Doubling the brightness feels like the same step whether you start in a dim room or in sunshine.Slide 15, “Neural processing”. For more on Weber's law and brightness, see NYU's Perception lecture notes on brightness.

Signals then travel along this path to the back of the brain:Slides 10 & 16, “Cortices”.

optic nerve→optic chiasm→optic tract→lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)→optic radiation→striate (visual) cortex

4 · The psychological model: the brain

The slide titled “Where are we?” sums up the whole system as lens → sensors → neurons & LGN → cortices. In the cortex, the occipital cortex handles patterns and the associative cortex handles links. We understand this stage least. The best way to study it is to watch where it goes wrong.

Mach bands: vision adds edges that aren't there

The real cross-section is a clean staircase. The perceived cross-section dips and overshoots at every step. Mach bands are not in the image. Your vision puts them there. The lecture explains them as a result of brightness adaptation, and the effect sharpens the edges we see.Slide 18. A clear write-up of the lateral-inhibition explanation: ACM SIGGRAPH HyperVis: Lateral Inhibition and Adaptation.

Edges: vision fills in what's missing

Most people read the broken strokes on slide 19 as the word HOT. Three “pacmen” arranged together make you see a white triangle that isn't drawn (the Kanizsa triangle). Vision completes missing edges using what it has learned. That shows the human eye needs training and can be deceived.

Static illusions: measurement needs comparison

  • Zöllner: the long lines are parallel, but the short slanted ticks make them look tilted.
  • Ebbinghaus: two identical centre circles look different sizes. The one surrounded by small circles looks bigger.

Both show that we judge size and angle relative to the surroundings, not absolutely.

Motion and colour illusions: complex function

The slides also show still patterns that seem to move (rotating snakes) and a ring of magenta dots around a cross. Try the ring below. Fix your eyes on the + for about 20 seconds.

+

A gap runs round the ring, and most people start to see a green dot travelling round. Then the magenta dots seem to fade away. Neither the green nor the fading is in the image. Benham's disk is a black-and-white disk that shows faint colours when it spins. The lecture's conclusion is that illusions are a consequence of complex function. The same processing that makes vision work so well is what produces them.

5 · So: is human vision a good model for computer vision?

The lecture's summary slide makes four points. (1) The eye can be modelled in three sections. (2) It works very well. (3) It can be deceived. (4) Is it a good model for computer vision? The lecture leaves the last one open, so be ready to argue both sides:

Yes: good inspirationNo: not a blueprint
It works very well. It is proof that robust vision is possible.It can be deceived (Mach bands, Zöllner, Ebbinghaus). A measuring system should not invent edges or misjudge sizes.
Some ideas carry over directly: three colour channels, compensating for weak blue, a log-like response, and emphasising edges.It isn't fully understood, especially the brain, so we can't copy what we don't understand.
Edges and context matter in human vision, which hints at what computer vision should look for.The hardware is different: a camera moves its lens, has no blind spot, and has a uniform pixel grid rather than a fovea.

A strong answer would say that human vision is a useful source of ideas, but it is not a specification to copy. Computer vision should borrow what works and avoid what deceives.

Exam-style recall

Write each answer in the box before you reveal the model answer.

Describe the three-part model of human vision (3 marks).
Physical model: the eye. The lens is shaped to focus an image on the retina and fovea, where it is sensed by rods and cones. Experimental model: the neural signals. Sensor responses pass through a log response, are weighted and combined, and are coded for the optic nerve. Psychological model: the brain. The cortices interpret the signals, with the occipital cortex handling patterns and the associative cortex handling links. This is the least understood part.
What are Mach bands, and what causes them? Sketch the actual and perceived cross-sections (4 marks).
Mach bands are bright and dark strips seen at the edges of uniform grey steps. They are not present in the image: the visual system introduces them through brightness adaptation. The actual cross-section is a clean staircase. The perceived one has an undershoot on the dark side and an overshoot on the bright side of each step, so edges look sharper than they are.
“Human vision is a good model for computer vision.” Discuss (6 marks).
For: it works very well and shows robust vision is possible. Useful ideas can be borrowed: three colour channels, compensation for the weak blue response, a log (Weber) response, and an emphasis on edges and context. Against: it can be deceived (Mach bands, Zöllner, Ebbinghaus, Benham), whereas a computer vision system often needs to measure accurately. It is not fully understood, especially the brain. The hardware also differs: the eye shapes its lens and has a fovea and a blind spot, while a camera moves its lens and has a uniform sensor. Conclusion: it is a valuable source of inspiration, but not a blueprint to copy.
